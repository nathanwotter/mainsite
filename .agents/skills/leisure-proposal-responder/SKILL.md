# Leisure proposal responder

Help Nathan draft individualized, warm, concise replies to student proposals. The output is for his review and posting. **Never click Post, submit a reply, approve an activity in Moodle, or change a grade.**

## Grounding

Read the current course's forum instructions before drafting; they override older examples. Read each original proposal and relevant follow-ups, then determine whether the activity is new to that student, engaging, outside their current competence/comfort zone, and addresses the wellness or personal benefit they named. Look for a specific plan to learn and try it. The Fall 2026 forum says shorter or on-campus activities must be completed at least three times and that students should wait for Nathan's approval before starting. Recheck these requirements if the course or semester changes.

Use [the de-identified response bank](references/response-bank.md) for Nathan's voice and recurring pedagogical judgments, not as a rigid policy. Older replies may set a more specific duration, cadence, class type, route, artifact, or evidence than the current instructions. Apply such specifics only when they fit the student's plan and Nathan's current expectations; flag uncertainty for review. Do not invent student facts, resources, due dates, accessibility requirements, or permission to start.

## Drafting and review

Work through each forum **oldest original proposal first**, using the original post date rather than the forum's last-activity sort or a recent follow-up date. Draft **one batch of up to 10 discussions at a time**. Present that batch for Nathan's review and stop; do not start the next batch until he asks. Note any discussion already handled by Nathan so it is not accidentally drafted again.

1. Make a local, durable review copy of each proposed reply **outside the Git-tracked skill/repo**. Include the discussion link, a short activity label, draft text, and any question or uncertainty for Nathan. Keep the batch in oldest-first order. Keep student names and proposal details out of the committed skill bank. A private, untracked local file is the source of truth, not Moodle's browser draft cache.
2. Usually acknowledge a specific strength or goal, then state whether the idea appears workable or what needs clarification/revision. Ask for only the decisive missing details (often novelty, one activity, learning structure, repeated attempts, or fit with leisure rather than another obligation). Give concrete, achievable suggestions without making the student's choice for them. Close in Nathan's collegial voice when natural.
3. Distinguish an apparent fit from final approval. If important details are missing, request a follow-up plan; do not imply approval. If the proposal is workable, draft an explicit approval only for Nathan to review and post.
4. Present a review queue with draft text and concise rationale/flags. Revise as Nathan directs. If asked to stage replies in Moodle, paste only reviewed text into the correct discussion's reply editor and leave it unposted. Never post, even when the text seems final.
5. Moodle may preserve separate unsent reply drafts across navigation, but that cache is not guaranteed durable. Keep the local review copy until Nathan confirms posting. When staging several replies, verify the intended text remains in the correct thread after navigation/refresh, and stop if an editor loses or cross-loads a draft. Do not rely on an autosave indicator alone. If staging is unreliable, give Nathan the review file and discussion links to paste/post one at a time.

When harvesting future examples, use Nathan's own replies only. Remove student and peer names, email addresses, IDs, discussion links, and identifying personal details before adding an example to the bank. Preserve the teaching move and voice; record semester and scenario only. Do not commit live student proposals or named draft queues to Git, even in a private repo.
